Telling the Ground Truth

A telling for the neighbors

Before I start: this isn't a Pomo story, and I'm not Pomo. I'm just a student of this valley. The people who kept this country longest understood something about poisoned ground that we forgot, and I want to borrow it for a few moments.

They knew that some dangerous places you can't just leave. Instead, you keep them. You teach the children the name. You say aloud, every generation: here is what happened here, and here is how to behave. The danger isn't fenced. It's remembered.

· · ·

So. Here's the ground.

For forty years there was a mill on the 266 acres south of town. Part of it pushed chemicals deep into wood so it wouldn't rot — copper, chromium, arsenic, and one called PCP, the kind that makes other poisons as it breaks down. Some of it went into the ground. A little every working day, by men doing their jobs exactly as they were trained.

Nobody broke a rule. The poison down there was made by people following the rules.

This past January, with no ceremony, the State closed the cleanup case. Not because it's clean—but because it can't be cleaned. Their plan is to leave it in the ground and watch it slowly dilute. The watching is the cleanup. And they wrote the warning into the deed—a covenant—and handed the whole job of remembering to a piece of paper in a drawer.

That's the thing I'm afraid of. Not the poison. The forgetting.

Because now someone wants to build a village on it. Maybe that's wonderful. I'm not here to say no.

I'm here to say what the old people would: know what's in the ground before you stand on it. Ask who keeps the record. Ask what "clean" was measured against. Ask who was at the table.

· · ·

This poison doesn't disappear. It changes shape. And the most dangerous shape it takes is the one that looks like a gift.

That's why I keep the chronicle. Not to stop anything—just to keep the ground told.
